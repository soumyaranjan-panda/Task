To attach a recording to any word, write `{{say:word~/ipa/~/audio/word.mp3}}` — the file plays when present and falls back to your device voice otherwise.

### How to read the symbols

Sounds are written between slashes, e.g. `/fəˈnetɪk/`. The **International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA)** gives one symbol per sound, so it works for any word. You do **not** need to memorize it — treat these tables as a lookup.

- `ˈ` = the next syllable is **stressed** (louder, longer): `banana` → /bəˈnɑːnə/ {{say:banana~/bəˈnɑːnə/~/audio/english/banana.mp3}}
- `ː` = the vowel is **long**: /iː/ (see) vs /ɪ/ (sit)

### Rhythm & intonation

English is **stress-timed**: content words (nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs) are strong, and function words (articles, prepositions, pronouns, auxiliaries) are weak and reduced to the schwa.

- Say it: `I can SEE the CAT.` {{say:I can see the cat~~/audio/english/i-can-see-the-cat.mp3}}
- The rhythm matters more than each sound: `THE PHOto is on the TAble.` {{say:The photo is on the table~~/audio/english/the-photo-is-on-the-table.mp3}}

Melody of a sentence:

- **Statements** fall at the end ↘ — `She is coming.` {{say:She is coming~~/audio/english/she-is-coming.mp3}}
- **Yes/No questions** rise ↗ — `Are you coming?` {{say:Are you coming~~/audio/english/are-you-coming.mp3}}
- **Wh-questions** fall ↘ — `Where are you going?` {{say:Where are you going~~/audio/english/where-are-you-going.mp3}}

## 2. Types of Sentences & Clauses

### What is a Sentence?

A sentence is a medium used to convey an idea, message, or information from one person to another.

**Example:** I want a glass of water.

The person you say this to understands you need water. You used a sentence to convey your message.

---

### What is a Clause?

A **clause** is a combination of words that contains **a subject AND a verb**. Both are required.

🟡 **UNDERSTAND:** A clause is just a mini-sentence. You don't need to memorize the formal definition. You just need to know that every clause needs a "who" (subject) and a "doing" (verb).

**Formula:**

> Clause = Subject + Verb (+ optional object/complement)

### Two Types of Clauses

| Type | Definition | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| **Independent Clause** | Makes complete sense on its own | *Ram enjoyed the movie.* |
| **Dependent Clause** | Does NOT make complete sense on its own | *because her father died* |

**Example showing both:**

> She is crying **because her father died.**

- "She is crying" = independent clause (complete sense)
- "because her father died" = dependent clause (incomplete on its own)

---

### Types of Sentences (by Purpose)

⚪ **DON'T MEMORIZE:** The names "declarative," "interrogative," "imperative," "exclamatory" are technical terms. You don't need to remember them. Just know what each type does.

#### 1. Declarative Sentence (Statement)
Makes a statement. Ends with a period.

> I am going to school.
> The book is on the table.

#### 2. Interrogative Sentence (Question)
Asks a question. Ends with a question mark.

> Are you coming to the party?
> Where is my phone?

#### 3. Imperative Sentence (Order/Request)
Gives a command, request, or instruction.

> Close the door.
> Please sit down.
> Don't touch that!

#### 4. Exclamatory Sentence (Emotion)
Expresses strong emotion. Ends with an exclamation mark.

> What a beautiful day!
> Alas, I failed my exam!

---

### How to Recognize a Clause

Ask yourself:
1. Is there a **subject** (who/what is doing something)?
2. Is there a **verb** (action or state of being)?

If both exist -> it is a clause.

> "running quickly" -> No subject -> NOT a clause
> "he runs" -> Has subject (he) + verb (runs) -> IS a clause

### Quick Way to Remember

Think of a clause as a **mini-sentence**. Every clause must have a "doer" (subject) and a "doing" (verb).

## 3. Parts of Speech

### Overview

There are **8 parts of speech** in English:

⚪ **DON'T MEMORIZE:** The formal names of all 8 parts of speech. You don't need to identify whether a word is a "noun" or "pronoun" in real life. Just understand what each type does.

| Part of Speech | What it does | Example |
|---------------|--------------|---------|
| Noun | Names a person, place, thing, or idea | *dog, love, Delhi* |
| Pronoun | Replaces a noun | *he, she, it, they* |
| Verb | Shows action or state of being | *run, is, think* |
| Adjective | Describes a noun | *beautiful, tall, red* |
| Adverb | Describes a verb, adjective, or another adverb | *quickly, very, here* |
| Conjunction | Connects words, phrases, or clauses | *and, but, because* |
| Preposition | Shows relationship (position/time) | *in, on, at, by* |
| Interjection | Expresses emotion | *wow, alas, oh* |

---

### 2.1 Nouns

A noun is a word that names a person, place, thing, or idea.

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|------|-----------|----------|
| Common Noun | General name | *boy, city, book* |
| Proper Noun | Specific name (capitalized) | *Rahul, Mumbai* |
| Abstract Noun | Ideas, feelings, qualities | *love, freedom, happiness* |
| Concrete Noun | Things you can see/touch | *table, water, music* |
| Collective Noun | Group of things | *team, flock, audience* |

---

### 2.2 Pronouns

A pronoun replaces a noun to avoid repetition.

🟡 **UNDERSTAND:** You don't need to memorize all the types of pronouns. Just know that pronouns replace nouns so you don't repeat yourself.

| Type | Examples | Usage |
|------|----------|-------|
| Personal | I, you, he, she, it, we, they | Refer to specific people/things |
| Possessive | mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs | Show ownership |
| Reflexive | myself, yourself, himself, herself | Refer back to the subject |
| Demonstrative | this, that, these, those | Point to specific things |
| Relative | who, whom, which, that | Connect clauses |
| Interrogative | who, what, which | Ask questions |

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER — Reflexive Pronouns:**

Use "myself/yourself/himself" ONLY when the subject and the object are the **same person**.

> I hurt **myself**. (I = subject, myself = object; same person -> correct)

⚡ **Practical Rule:**

> **"Myself" is NOT for introductions.**
> Say "I am Raj" NOT "Myself Raj."

See [Common Mistakes](#10-common-grammar-mistakes) for more details.

---

### 2.3 Verbs

A verb shows action or a state of being.

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|------|-----------|----------|
| Action Verb | Shows physical or mental action | *run, think, write* |
| Linking Verb | Connects subject to description | *is, am, are, was, were, seem* |
| Helping/Auxiliary Verb | Helps the main verb | *is, have, will, can, do* |
| Modal Verb | Shows possibility, ability, permission | *can, could, may, might, will* |

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER — Verb Forms:**

You need to know these forms for passive voice and tenses:

| Form | Name | Example (take) |
|------|------|----------------|
| take | Base form / Infinitive | take |
| took | Simple Past (V2) | took |
| taken | Past Participle (V3) | taken |
| taking | Present Participle (-ing) | taking |

⚪ **DON'T MEMORIZE:** The formal names like "infinitive," "present participle." Just know the forms: base, past, V3, -ing.

---

### 2.4 Adjectives

Adjectives **describe or modify nouns**. They tell you more about a person, place, or thing.

**Formula:** Adjective + Noun

**Examples:**

- She is **beautiful**. (describes "she")
- He bought a **red** car. (describes "car")
- I have **three** books. (describes quantity)

**Additional examples (from transcript):**

- The **tall** man walked in.
- It was a **long** day.
- She wore a **silk** dress.

### How to Recognize Adjectives

Ask: **What kind? How many? Which one?**

> "What kind of car?" -> A **red** car.
> "How many books?" -> **Three** books.
> "Which dress?" -> The **silk** dress.

---

### 2.5 Adverbs

Adverbs **describe verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs**. They often end in "-ly" but not always.

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER — Adjective vs Adverb:**

> **Adjective** = describes a **noun** (a beautiful day)
> **Adverb** = describes a **verb** (she sings beautifully)

⚡ **Practical Rule:**

> After a verb, use an **-ly** word (adverb), not a plain adjective.
> "She sings **beautifully**" NOT "She sings beautiful."

**Key Rule:** An adverb tells you **how, when, where, or to what extent** something happens.

**Structure from transcript:**

> Subject + Verb + Adverb

**Examples from transcript:**

| Sentence | Adverb | What it modifies |
|----------|--------|-----------------|
| He is driving slowly. | *slowly* | the verb *driving* |
| They will come tomorrow. | *tomorrow* | the verb *come* |
| He is extremely angry. | *extremely* | the adjective *angry* |

**Additional examples:**

- She sings **beautifully**. (modifies the verb "sings")
- He is **very** tall. (modifies the adjective "tall")
- She finished **quickly**. (modifies the verb "finished")
- He ran **extremely** fast. (modifies the adverb "fast")

### Adverbs Can Modify Other Adverbs

> She drives **very slowly**.

- "slowly" = adverb modifying "drives"
- "very" = adverb modifying "slowly"

### Common Adverb-Adjective Combinations

| Adverb | Adjective | Example |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| very | tall | He is **very** tall. |
| extremely | angry | She is **extremely** angry. |
| quite | good | The movie was **quite** good. |
| really | fast | He runs **really** fast. |

### How to Recognize Adverbs

Ask: **How? When? Where? To what extent?**

> "How does she sing?" -> She sings **beautifully**.
> "When will they come?" -> They will come **tomorrow**.
> "How angry is he?" -> He is **extremely** angry.

### 2.6 Interjections

Interjections **express emotions** and are followed by an **exclamation mark (!)**.

**Examples from transcript:**

> **Alas!** I failed in my exams. (sadness)
> **Wow!** What a beautiful day! (excitement)
> **Oh!** I didn't see you there. (surprise)
> **Bravo!** You did it! (appreciation)

### How to Recognize Interjections

- They stand alone or are at the beginning of a sentence
- They express sudden emotion
- They are followed by an exclamation mark

### Note

In formal writing, interjections are rarely used. They are common in:
- Casual conversation
- Dialogue in stories
- Exclamatory sentences

---

### 2.7 Prepositions

Prepositions show the **relationship** between a noun/pronoun and other words in a sentence. They tell you **position** or **time**.

**Two Main Categories:**

| Category | Tells you | Examples |
|----------|----------|----------|
| Place | **Where** something is | in, on, at, under, between, behind |
| Time | **When** something happens | at, on, in, since, for, until |

**Examples from transcript:**

| Sentence | Preposition | Category | What it tells |
|----------|-------------|----------|---------------|
| She is **in** the room. | *in* | Place | Where she is |
| Ram came **out of** the room. | *out of* | Place | Position of Ram |
| I will meet him **at** 5 PM. | *at* | Time | When the meeting happens |
| Keys are **on** the table. | *on* | Place | Where the keys are |

**Additional examples:**

- The cat is **under** the table. (place)
- I have been working **since** morning. (time)
- The movie starts **at** 7 PM. (time)
- The book is **between** the two boxes. (place)

### Common Prepositions

**Place:** in, on, at, above, below, under, between, behind, near, beside, across, through, over, to, from

**Time:** at, on, in, since, for, until, till, during, before, after

### Important Note

The prepositions **in, on, and at** are used for BOTH place and time, but the usage differs:

| Preposition | Place Example | Time Example |
|-------------|--------------|--------------|
| **in** | in the room | in January / in 2025 |
| **on** | on the table | on Monday / on March 5th |
| **at** | at the door | at 5 PM / at noon |

### Quick Way to Remember (Place)

Think from **big to small:**

> **in** = inside a space (big) -> in the city, in the room
> **on** = on a surface -> on the table, on the wall
> **at** = at a specific point (small) -> at the door, at the corner

### Quick Way to Remember (Time)

> **in** = months, years, seasons, centuries -> in January, in 2025
> **on** = days, dates -> on Monday, on 5th March
> **at** = exact time -> at 9 AM, at noon, at midnight

## 4. Conjunctions

### What are Conjunctions?

Conjunctions are words that **connect** two sentences, clauses, or words together.

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:** The 7 coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS): **for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so**

**Examples from transcript:**

> She wants an ice cream **and** an apple.
> (connects two nouns: "an ice cream" and "an apple")

> I went out to play **but** it started raining.
> (connects two clauses: "I went out to play" and "it started raining")

---

### Three Types of Conjunctions

### 3.1 Coordinating Conjunctions

These connect **two independent clauses** (both make complete sense on their own).

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER — FANBOYS:**

| Letter | Conjunction | Function |
|--------|------------|----------|
| F | for | shows reason |
| A | and | adds information |
| N | nor | adds negative information |
| B | but | shows contrast |
| O | or | shows choice/alternative |
| Y | yet | shows contrast (unexpected) |
| S | so | shows result |

**Formula:**

> Independent Clause + FANBOYS + Independent Clause

**Examples:**

> He is good at singing **but** he lacks practice.
> It was his birthday **so** we went out for dinner.
> I wanted to go **but** it was raining.
> She likes tea **or** coffee. (connecting words, not full clauses here)

**How to Recognize:**

- Both parts before and after the conjunction can stand alone as complete sentences
- The conjunction connects equal ideas

---

### 3.2 Subordinating Conjunctions

These connect a **dependent clause** to an **independent clause**. One part depends on the other.

🟡 **UNDERSTAND:** Any conjunction that is NOT a FANBOYS word is a subordinating conjunction. You don't need to memorize the full list.

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:** The most common ones: **because, although, though, if, unless, since, when, while, until**

**Memory Trick: "Short White Bus"**

| Letter | Conjunction |
|--------|------------|
| S | since |
| H | how |
| O | although |
| R | (remember) |
| T | though |
| W | when / while / whenever |
| H | however |
| I | if / even if |
| T | till / until |
| E | unless |
| B | because |
| U | (remember) |
| S | (remember) |

**Important rule from transcript:** Any conjunction that is NOT a FANBOYS word is a subordinating conjunction.

**Examples:**

> We cancelled our plan **because** it was raining.
> ("We cancelled our plan" = independent; "because it was raining" = dependent)

> **Since** you came an hour early, you have to wait.
> ("you came an hour early" = dependent; "you have to wait" = independent)

**More subordinating conjunctions:**

although, though, even though, while, when, whenever, before, after, since, because, if, unless, until, till, as, as if, as though, so that, in order that

---

### 3.3 Correlative Conjunctions

These come in **pairs** and connect equal elements.

| Pair | Usage | Example |
|------|-------|---------|
| either...or | choice between two | Give me **either** an apple **or** a banana. |
| neither...nor | negative choice | Give me **neither** an apple **nor** a banana. |
| both...and | addition | Give me **both** apple **and** banana. |
| not only...but also | emphasis on both | She is **not only** smart **but also** hardworking. |
| whether...or | choice/uncertainty | I don't know **whether** to stay **or** leave. |

---

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER — Common Conjunction Mistakes:**

| Mistake | Correction | Why |
|---------|-----------|-----|
| "Although it was raining, but I went out." | "Although it was raining, I went out." | Don't use both "although" and "but" |
| "Because I was tired, so I slept." | "Because I was tired, I slept." | Don't use both "because" and "so" |

⚡ **Practical Rule:**

> **Never use two conjunctions from the same category together.**
> Although...but ❌ | Because...so ❌ | If...then ❌

---

### Comparison Table: Types of Conjunctions

| Feature | Coordinating | Subordinating | Correlative |
|---------|-------------|---------------|-------------|
| What it connects | Two independent clauses | Dependent + Independent clause | Equal elements |
| Memory trick | FANBOYS | Short White Bus | Pairs |
| Examples | and, but, so | because, although, if | either...or, both...and |
| Clause relationship | Equal | One depends on other | Equal |

## 5. Active Voice and Passive Voice

### What is Voice?

Voice tells us whether the **subject performs the action** or **receives the action**.

🟡 **UNDERSTAND:** Active voice = you do the action. Passive voice = action happens to you. That's all you need to know conceptually.

---

### 4.1 Active Voice

**Definition:** The subject **performs** the action stated by the verb.

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER — Active Voice Structure:**

> Subject + Verb + Object

**Example:**

> **She** eats **an apple**.

- She = subject (doing the eating)
- eats = verb
- an apple = object (receiving the action)

**More examples:**

> The cat chased the mouse.
> I wrote a letter.
> They are building a house.
> She reads books every day.

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:** Use active voice in everyday conversation. It's more direct and clear.

---

### 4.2 Passive Voice

**Definition:** The subject **is acted upon** by the verb. The subject receives the action instead of performing it.

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER — Passive Voice Structure:**

> Object + be (in correct tense) + V3 (past participle) + by + doer

**Example:**

> **An apple** is eaten **by her**.

- An apple = object (now in subject position)
- is eaten = verb in passive form (to be + V3)
- her = original subject (now in object position with "by")

**More examples:**

> The mouse was chased by the cat.
> A letter was written by me.
> A house is being built by them.
> Books are read by her every day.

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER — When to Use Passive Voice:**

The transcript identifies **three important cases:**

**Case 1: When the subject is unknown or unimportant**

> The letter is written. (Who wrote it? We don't know or care.)

**Additional examples:**

> The road was damaged in the flood.
> Mistakes were made.

**Case 2: When the subject is less important than the action**

> The rat is chased by the cat.

Here, the focus is on what happens to the rat, not on the cat doing the chasing.

**Additional examples:**

> The decision was announced yesterday.
> New rules have been implemented.

**Case 3: When we don't want to mention the subject at all**

> The rat is chased and it falls in the well and died.

We don't mention "by the cat" because we don't want to identify the doer.

**Additional examples:**

> The window was broken. (We don't say "by someone.")
> He was arrested. (We don't say "by the police.")

---

### Rules for Converting Active to Passive Voice

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER — 3 Steps to Convert Active to Passive:**

**Rule 1: Move object to subject position**

Active: She eats an apple.
Passive: An apple ________ by her.

**Rule 2: Use the past participle (V3) of the main verb with an auxiliary verb**

Active: She **eats** an apple.
Passive: An apple **is eaten** by her.

The tense of the auxiliary verb matches the original tense:

| Active Tense | Passive Tense | Example (Active) | Example (Passive) |
|-------------|---------------|------------------|-------------------|
| Simple Present | is/am/are + V3 | She eats. | It is eaten. |
| Simple Past | was/were + V3 | She ate. | It was eaten. |
| Present Continuous | is/am/are + being + V3 | She is eating. | It is being eaten. |
| Present Perfect | has/have + been + V3 | She has eaten. | It has been eaten. |
| Simple Future | will + be + V3 | She will eat. | It will be eaten. |
| Modal | modal + be + V3 | She can eat. | It can be eaten. |

**Rule 3: Use "by" before the original subject**

> She eats an apple -> An apple is eaten **by** her.

**Important:** "By" is a preposition that always comes BEFORE the subject in passive voice. You cannot place it anywhere else.

---

### Special Cases: Using "with" or "to" Instead of "by"

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:** In some cases, you CANNOT use "by." You must use "with" or "to."

**Use "to" when:**

> She knows him -> He is known **to** her. (NOT "by her")

Other examples:

> He is married **to** her. (NOT "by her")
> The answer is known **to** everyone. (NOT "by everyone")

**Use "with" when:**

> Water fills the glass -> The glass is filled **with** water. (NOT "by water")

Other examples:

> The floor is covered **with** snow.
> The bag is stuffed **with** clothes.

⚡ **Practical Rule:**

> **by** = the doer performs the action (written by her)
> **to** = the action relates to a person (known to him, married to her)
> **with** = the material/instrument used (filled with water, covered with snow)

---

### Conversion Chart: Active to Passive

| Active | Passive |
|--------|---------|
| She eats an apple | An apple is eaten by her |
| They built the house | The house was built by them |
| He is writing a letter | A letter is being written by him |
| She has completed the work | The work has been completed by her |
| They will announce the results | The results will be announced by them |
| The teacher may punish the student | The student may be punished by the teacher |

---

### Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Correction | Why |
|---------|-----------|-----|
| "The apple is eat by her." | "The apple is **eaten** by her." | Must use past participle (V3), not base form |
| "She is known by everyone." | "She is known **to** everyone." | Some verbs use "to" instead of "by" |
| "The glass is filled by water." | "The glass is filled **with** water." | Use "with" for materials/substances |
| "An apple eaten by her." | "An apple **is** eaten by her." | Need auxiliary verb (is/was/etc.) |

## 6. Direct and Indirect Speech (Reported Speech)

### What is Speech?

Speech is the medium we use to convey a message from one person to another. There are two ways to report what someone said.

🟡 **UNDERSTAND:** Direct speech = someone's exact words. Indirect speech = reporting what someone said in your own words.

---

### 5.1 Direct Speech

**Definition:** When you convey the message **in the actual words of the speaker**, using quotation marks.

**Example:**

> Ram said, "I am not happy."

- "I am not happy" = the actual words of Ram (the speaker)

**How to Recognize:**

- Always has **quotation marks** (" ")
- Always has a **comma** before the quoted words
- The words inside the quotes are exactly what the speaker said

**Structure:**

> Reporting clause + , + "Reported clause"

---

### 5.2 Indirect Speech (Reported Speech)

**Definition:** When you convey the message **in your own words** without changing the meaning.

**Example:**

> Ram said that he was not happy.

- "he was not happy" = your words conveying Ram's message
- The meaning is preserved, but the exact words are changed

**Three Important Facts:**

1. You can convey the message in your own words
2. You must not change the meaning of what the speaker said
3. The punctuation marks (comma and quotes) are removed

---

### Components of Direct Speech

In the sentence: Ram said, "I am not happy."

| Component | Part | Description |
|-----------|------|-------------|
| **Reporting Clause** | Ram said | The part that reports what was said |
| **Reporting Verb** | said | The verb in the reporting clause |
| **Reported Clause** | "I am not happy" | The actual words spoken |
| **Reported Verb** | am | The verb inside the reported clause |

---

### Rules for Converting Direct to Indirect Speech

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER — 4 Rules for Converting Direct to Indirect Speech:**

### Rule 1: Add "that" and Remove Punctuation Marks

The comma and quotation marks are replaced with the connector **"that"**.

**Direct:** Ram said, "I am not happy."
**Indirect:** Ram said **that** he was not happy.

| Direct | Indirect |
|--------|---------|
| Ram said**,** "**I am not happy.**" | Ram said **that** he was not happy. |

---

### Rule 2: Change the Pronoun

The pronoun in the reported clause must change to match the person being reported about.

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:** I -> he/she, my -> his/her, we -> they

| Direct Speech | Indirect Speech |
|--------------|----------------|
| I | he/she |
| my | his/her |
| me | him/her |
| we | they |
| our | their |
| you | he/she/they (depends on context) |

**Example:**

> Ram said, "**I** am not happy." -> Ram said that **he** was not happy.

---

### Rule 3: Change the Tense

When the reporting verb is in the **past tense** (said, told, asked), the tense of the reported clause shifts one step back into the past.

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:** Everything moves one step into the past.

**Exception:** If the reporting verb is in the **present tense** (says), the tense does NOT change.

**Direct:** Ram said, "I am not happy." (said = past)
**Indirect:** Ram said that he **was** not happy. (am -> was)

**Direct:** Ram says, "I am not happy." (says = present)
**Indirect:** Ram says that he **is** not happy. (stays present)

---

### Tense Conversion Chart

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:** Everything moves one step into the past.

| Direct Speech Tense | Indirect Speech Tense | Example (Direct) | Example (Indirect) |
|--------------------|----------------------|------------------|-------------------|
| Simple Present | Simple Past | "I cook every day" | He cooked every day |
| Present Continuous | Past Continuous | "I am cooking" | He was cooking |
| Simple Past | Past Perfect | "I cooked" | He had cooked |
| Present Perfect | Past Perfect | "I have cooked" | He had cooked |
| Past Continuous | Past Perfect Continuous | "I was cooking" | He had been cooking |
| Will | Would | "I will cook" | He would cook |
| Can | Could | "I can cook" | He could cook |
| May | Might | "I may cook" | He might cook |

**Memory Trick:** Everything moves one step into the past.

**Exception:** If the reporting verb is in present or future tense, the tense does NOT change.

> Ram **says**, "I am happy" -> Ram says that he **is** happy. (no change)
> Ram **will say**, "I am happy" -> Ram will say that he **is** happy. (no change)

---

### Rule 4: Change Time Words

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:** Time expressions must also change to reflect the shift.

| Direct Speech | Indirect Speech |
|--------------|----------------|
| today | that day |
| tomorrow | the next day / the following day |
| yesterday | the previous day / the day before |
| tonight | that night |
| now | then |
| here | there |
| this | that |
| these | those |
| ago | before |
| last night | the previous night |

**Example:**

> Ram said, "I am meeting my friend **today**."
> Ram said that he was meeting his friend **that day**.

---

### Complete Conversion Example

**Direct:** Ram said, "I am meeting my friend today."

**Step-by-step conversion:**

| Rule | Application |
|------|-------------|
| Rule 1: Add "that", remove punctuation | Ram said that I am meeting my friend today |
| Rule 2: Change pronoun (I -> he) | Ram said that he is meeting my friend today |
| Rule 3: Change tense (am meeting -> was meeting) | Ram said that he was meeting my friend today |
| Rule 4: Change time word (today -> that day) | Ram said that he was meeting his friend that day |

**Final Indirect:** Ram said that he was meeting his friend that day.

---

### 5.3 Special Cases in Indirect Speech

### Imperative Sentences (Orders/Requests)

When the direct speech is an order or request, the conversion is different.

**Formula:**

> Reporting verb + to/not to + base verb

**Examples:**

| Direct | Indirect |
|--------|---------|
| Ram said, "Close the window." | Ram **ordered** me to close the window. |
| Ram said, "Please help me." | Ram **requested** me to help him. |
| Ram said, "Don't go there." | Ram **told** me **not to** go there. |

**Reporting verbs for imperatives:**

- told
- ordered
- requested
- asked
- advised
- suggested
- urged

**Choosing the right verb depends on the tone:**

> "Close the door!" -> He **ordered** me to close the door. (firm tone)
> "Please close the door." -> He **requested** me to close the door. (polite tone)

---

### Interrogative Sentences (Questions)

Questions are converted differently depending on their type.

**Type 1: Yes/No Questions (use "if" or "whether")**

| Direct | Indirect |
|--------|---------|
| Ram said, "**Have** you prepared food?" | Ram asked **if** I **had** prepared food. |

**Key changes:**
- "said to" -> "asked"
- comma + quotes -> "if" or "whether"
- "have" -> "had" (tense shift)
- "you" -> "I" (pronoun change)
- Question becomes statement (no question mark)

**Type 2: WH-Questions (use the WH-word)**

| Direct | Indirect |
|--------|---------|
| Ram said, "When **do** you study?" | Ram asked when I **studied**. |
| Ram said, "Where **do** you live?" | Ram asked where I **lived**. |
| Ram said, "What **are** you doing?" | Ram asked what I **was doing**. |

**Key changes:**
- "said to" -> "asked"
- WH-word remains (when, where, what, why, how)
- "do/does" is removed
- Main verb shifts to past tense
- Question becomes statement

**Type 3: Questions with Modals (will, can, may, etc.)**

| Direct | Indirect |
|--------|---------|
| Ram said, "**Will** you go to the movie?" | Ram asked **if** I **would** go to the movie. |

---

### Exclamatory Sentences

When the direct speech is an exclamation or expression of surprise.

**Formula:**

> Reporting verb + exclaim that + past tense

**Example:**

> Ram said, "Wow! What a beautiful day this is!"
> Ram **exclaimed that** it was a beautiful day.

---

### Modal Verbs in Indirect Speech

| Direct | Indirect | Note |
|--------|---------|------|
| can | could | always changes |
| may | might | always changes |
| must | had to | changes (past of must = had to) |
| will | would | always changes |
| shall | should | always changes |
| should | should | can stay the same |
| could | could | stays the same |
| might | might | stays the same |

**Example:**

> Ram said, "I **can** sing." -> Ram said that he **could** sing.
> Ram said, "I **must** sing every day." -> Ram said that he **had to** sing every day.

**Important note about "must":**

The past form of "must" (when expressing compulsion) is "had to."

> "I must finish this" -> He said he **had to** finish this.

---

### Common Mistakes in Indirect Speech

| Mistake | Correction | Why |
|---------|-----------|-----|
| "Ram said that I am happy." | "Ram said that **he** was happy." | Must change pronoun and tense |
| "Ram asked that where I lived." | "Ram asked where I lived." | Don't use "that" with WH-questions |
| "Ram told me close the door." | "Ram told me **to** close the door." | Need "to" for imperatives |
| "He said me that..." | "He **told** me that..." or "He said that..." | "Said" doesn't take an object directly |

## 7. Modal Verbs: Can and Could

### What are Modal Verbs?

Modal verbs (also called modal auxiliaries or modals) are special helping verbs that express **ability, possibility, permission, obligation**, or other meanings.

🟡 **UNDERSTAND:** You don't need to memorize that these are called "modal auxiliaries." Just know they're special helping verbs that add meaning like ability, permission, possibility.

**The 10 Modal Verbs:**

> can, could, may, might, will, would, shall, should, must, ought to

---

### 6.1 CAN

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:** Use "can" for: ability, permission, offers, and informal orders.

**Uses of "can" (from transcript):**

#### Use 1: Ability

Talking about what someone is able to do (present).

> I **can** read English but I **can't** speak it.
> Cats **can't** bark like dogs.
> **Can** this chair fold?

**Additional examples:**

> She can play the piano.
> He can speak three languages.
> I can't swim very well.

**Structure:**

> Subject + can/can't + base verb

---

#### Use 2: Common or Typical Things

Something that happens regularly or is generally true.

> Delhi **can** be very cold in December.
> She **can** be happy just by talking to me.
> People **can** be influenced by you.

**Additional examples:**

> The weather can be unpredictable here.
> Exams can be stressful.
> Children can be very noisy.

**How to Recognize:** The situation described is something that happens occasionally or is generally possible.

---

#### Use 3: Possibility at Some Moment

Something is possible, but not always.

> We **can** go for dinner tonight.
> I **can't** come to meet you today.
> We **can** try asking him for money.

**Additional examples:**

> We can visit them this weekend.
> I can help you with that tomorrow.
> The train can be delayed sometimes.

**How to Recognize:** The possibility is limited to a specific time or situation.

---

#### Use 4: Asking and Giving Permission

> **Can** I ask you something? (asking)
> Yes, you **can**. (giving)
> **Can** I have more wine? (asking)
> No, you **can't**. (refusing)

**Additional examples:**

> Can I use your phone?
> Can we leave early today?
> You can take my car.

**Structure (asking):**

> Can + subject + base verb?

**Structure (giving permission):**

> Yes, you can. / No, you can't.

---

#### Use 5: Offering Help

> **Can** I help you?
> I **can** write the essay for you.
> We **can** bring the food when coming.

**Additional examples:**

> Can I carry that for you?
> I can explain this to the class.
> We can pick you up from the airport.

---

#### Use 6: Orders (Informal)

Used with close friends/family. Can sound impolite with strangers.

> **Can** you put it down?
> **Can** you not do that?
> **Can** we leave now?

**Additional examples:**

> Can you close the door?
> Can you be quiet for a minute?
> Can you pass me the salt?

**Note:** In formal situations, use "Could you..." instead of "Can you..." for orders/requests.

---

#### Use 7: Verbs of Senses (see, hear, smell, taste)

These sense verbs CANNOT be used in continuous form (-ing). Use "can" or the simple form instead.

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| I am seeing him run. | I **can see** him run. / I see him run. |
| I am hearing a knock. | I **can hear** a knock. / I hear a knock. |
| Are you tasting the ginger? | **Can** you taste the ginger? |

**Important:** These sentences ARE continuous in meaning even though they don't use "-ing." The activity is happening now.

**Additional examples:**

> I can smell something burning.
> Can you feel the wind?
> I can taste garlic in this soup.

---

#### Use 8: Chances/Probability (INCORRECT usage)

"Can" CANNOT be used to express probability or chances about future events.

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| There **can** be** a seminar next week. | There **may/might** be a seminar next week. |
| We **can** go running tomorrow. | We **may/might** go running tomorrow. |

**Why?** "Can" expresses ability or general possibility, not specific future probability. Use "may" or "might" for chances.

---

### 6.2 COULD

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:** Use "could" for: past ability, polite requests, and criticism (could have + V3).

**Uses of "could" (from transcript):**

#### Use 1: Past Ability

Talking about what someone was able to do in the past.

> I **could** read English when I was five.
> My father **could** run 10 kilometers in a go.
> Two years ago, I **could** code.

**Additional examples:**

> She could speak French when she was young.
> I could run very fast as a teenager.
> He could play guitar before his accident.

**Structure:**

> Subject + could + base verb

**Note:** "Could" for past ability implies the ability may or may not exist now.

---

#### Use 2: Polite Version of "Can"

Using "could" makes requests and offers sound more polite.

| Direct (can) | Polite (could) |
|-------------|----------------|
| We can go running tomorrow. | We **could** go running tomorrow. |
| You can get a better offer. | You **could** get a better offer. |

**Additional examples:**

> Could you help me with this?
> Could I borrow your pen?
> You could try restarting the computer.

---

#### Use 3: You Could But You Didn't (Missed Opportunity)

Used to talk about something that was possible but didn't happen. Always use **"could have"** + past participle.

> I **could have** made it anytime I wanted. (But I didn't.)
> I **could have** won if I hadn't fallen. (But I fell.)

**Additional examples:**

> She could have passed if she had studied harder.
> We could have caught the train if we had left earlier.
> He could have told me about the meeting.

**Structure:**

> Subject + could have + past participle (V3)

**Important:** "Could have" is ALWAYS followed by the past participle because of "have."

---

#### Use 4: Criticism

Used to express frustration or criticize someone for not doing something they were able to do.

> You **could have** helped me. (You were able to help, but you didn't.)
> You **could have** told me about that. (You should have informed me.)

**Additional examples:**

> You could have called me before coming.
> They could have finished the project on time.
> You could have at least warned me.

**Structure (for criticism):**

> Subject + could have + past participle

---

#### Use 5: Permission (Polite)

A more polite version of asking/giving permission.

> **Could** I ask you something?
> **Could** you come to pick me up?

**Additional examples:**

> Could I use the restroom?
> Could we extend the deadline?
> Could I have a glass of water?

---

#### Use 6: Requests (Polite)

The polite version of ordering is called a request.

> **Could** you lend me some money?
> Do you think you **could** help me?

**Additional examples:**

> Could you open the window, please?
> Could you repeat that, please?
> Do you think you could finish this by tomorrow?

---

#### Use 7: Indirect Speech

When converting direct speech with "can" to indirect speech, use "could."

**Direct:** She said, "Can you help me?"
**Indirect:** She asked if I **could** help her.

---

#### Use 8: NOT for Past Events That Happened Once

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:** "Could" cannot be used for something that happened just once in the past. Use "managed to" or "were able to" instead.

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| I **could** throw the stick 10 feet yesterday. | I **managed to** throw the stick 10 feet yesterday. |
| How many chocolates **could** you get? | How many chocolates **were you able to** get? |

**Why?** "Could" is for general or repeated past ability. For a specific one-time achievement, use "managed to" or "were able to."

**Additional examples:**

> After many attempts, I **was able to** solve the puzzle. (one-time)
> She **managed to** finish the race despite the injury. (one-time)

---

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER — CAN vs COULD:**

| Feature | CAN | COULD |
|---------|-----|-------|
| Time | Present | Past |
| Politeness | Normal | More polite |
| Missed opportunity | No | could have + V3 |
| One-time past event | No | No (use managed to) |
| Example | I can swim. | I could swim when I was 5. |

## 8. Modal Verbs: May and Might

### 7.1 MAY

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:** Use "may" for: chances, permission (I/we only), and contrasting ideas.

**Uses of "may" (from transcript):**

#### Use 1: Chances/Probability (Present and Future)

There is a possibility, but no guarantee.

> We **may** go hiking today.
> You **may** be right about this.

**Higher chances:** Add "well" to increase certainty.

> You **may well** be right about this.

**Additional examples:**

> It may rain tomorrow.
> She may come to the party.
> The train may be delayed.

**Structure:**

> Subject + may + base verb

---

#### Use 2: Contrasting Ideas

Used to show two contrasting or opposite ideas, usually with "but."

> He **may** be strong, but he won't win.
> You **may** be my superior, but that doesn't mean you're better than me.

**Additional examples:**

> She may be rich, but she's not happy.
> The food may look good, but it doesn't taste great.
> He may be smart, but he's lazy.

**Structure:**

> Subject + may + be + adjective, but + contrasting clause

---

#### Use 3: "May as well" (Nothing Better to Do)

Used when there's no better alternative. Always use with "as well."

> There's no one here. We **may as well** go home.
> It is too late. We **may as well** have something to eat.

**Additional examples:**

> Nobody's coming. We may as well cancel the meeting.
> It's already broken. You may as well throw it away.
> The movie is terrible. We may as well leave.

**Structure:**

> Subject + may as well + base verb

**How to Recognize:** The situation implies "since nothing better is available, we might as well do this."

---

#### Use 4: Permission (First Person Only)

"May" can be used to ask or give permission, but ONLY with **I** and **we** (first person).

> **May** I come in? (correct - first person)
> **May** we leave now? (correct - first person)

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| May you leave now? | Could you leave now? |
| May he come in? | Can/Could he come in? |

**Why?** "May" for permission is formal and traditionally used only with first person.

**Additional examples:**

> May I sit here?
> May I ask you a question?
> May we begin the presentation?

---

#### Use 5: NOT for Direct Questions About Chance

"May" cannot be used in direct questions asking about probability.

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| **May** you go shopping today? | **Are you likely to** go shopping today? |
| **May** it rain tomorrow? | **Is it likely to** rain tomorrow? |

**Exception:** In indirect questions, "may" CAN be used.

> Do you think you **may** go shopping? (correct - indirect question)

**Additional examples:**

> Do you think it may snow?
> I wonder if she may join us.
> I'm not sure if he may be available.

---

### 7.2 MIGHT

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:** "Might" = smaller chance than "may." Also use "might have + V3" for things that were possible but didn't happen.

**Uses of "might" (from transcript):**

#### Use 1: Smaller Chance Than "May"

"Might" expresses a lower probability than "may."

| Modal | Chance Level | Example |
|-------|-------------|---------|
| may | ~50% | I may go to Pune today. |
| might | ~30% | He might come with us. |

**Additional examples:**

> It might rain later. (less certain than "may rain")
> She might come to the party. (not very likely)
> We might win the lottery. (very unlikely)

**Structure:**

> Subject + might + base verb

---

#### Use 2: Conditional Meaning (If...then...)

Used in conditional sentences where one action depends on another.

> If you rest for an hour, you **might** feel better.
> Don't play with fire; you **might** burn yourself.

**Additional examples:**

> If you study hard, you might pass the exam.
> If we leave now, we might reach on time.
> If you eat too much, you might feel sick.

**Structure:**

> If + condition, subject + might + base verb

---

#### Use 3: Indirect Speech

When converting direct speech with "may" to indirect speech, use "might."

**Direct:** She said, "I may go to Goa."
**Indirect:** She said she **might** go to Goa.

**Additional examples:**

> Direct: "I may be late." -> Indirect: He said he might be late.
> Direct: "It may not work." -> Indirect: She said it might not work.

---

#### Use 4: Something That Was Possible But Didn't Happen

Used with "have" + past participle to talk about missed possibilities.

> You **might have** killed yourself! (You didn't, but it was possible.)
> If you hadn't been so annoying, I **might have** dated you. (But I didn't.)

**Additional examples:**

> She might have won if she had trained harder.
> We might have caught the bus if we had left earlier.
> He might have become a great player.

**Structure:**

> Subject + might have + past participle (V3)

---

#### Use 5: Permission (Very Formal)

"May" is more common for formal permission, but "might" can also be used in very formal contexts.

> I wonder if I **might** have a little more wine.
> **Might** I have a little more wine?

**Note:** This sounds very formal. In everyday English, use "could" or "may."

---

#### Use 6: True But Makes No Difference

Used with "although," "though," or "even if" to show that something is true but irrelevant.

> She **might** be nice, but she didn't win.
> He **might** be talented, but he failed.

**Additional examples:**

> He might be rich, but he's not generous.
> She might be intelligent, but she has no common sense.
> The car might be expensive, but it's not worth it.

**Replaceable with:** Although, though, even if

> **Although** he is strong, he didn't win.
> **Though** she is talented, she failed.
> **Even if** he is smart, it doesn't matter.

---

#### Use 7: Criticism

Similar to "could have," used to criticize someone.

> You **might** ask before you take my car.
> He **might have** told me that he was going to be late.

**Additional examples:**

> You might have warned me about the traffic.
> She might have called to say she'd be late.
> They might have finished the work by now.

---

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER — MAY vs MIGHT:**

| Feature | MAY | MIGHT |
|---------|-----|-------|
| Chance level | Higher (~50%) | Lower (~30%) |
| Missed possibility | No | might have + V3 |
| Formal permission | Yes (I/we) | Very formal |
| Example | It may rain. | It might rain. |

**Memory Trick:** "May" = more likely. "Might" = less likely (the "t" at the end makes it "tighter" or more uncertain).

## 9. Modal Verbs: Will and Would

### 8.1 WILL

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:** Use "will" for: future actions, certainty, willingness, and informal requests.

**Uses of "will" (from transcript):**

#### Use 1: Future Auxiliary (Future Tense)

Used to form future tense sentences (simple or perfect).

> I **will** be happy when my exams are over. (simple future)
> This time tomorrow, I**'ll** be at home. (future continuous idea)
> She **will** have finished the project by this evening. (future perfect)

**Additional examples:**

> They will arrive tomorrow.
> I will call you later.
> We will have eaten by 8 PM.

**Structure:**

> Subject + will + base verb (simple future)
> Subject + will have + past participle (future perfect)

---

#### Use 2: Certainty or Confidence

When you are sure about something.

> I'm sure you **will** understand this.
> Tomorrow **will** be a pleasant day.
> There's someone at the door. That **will** be my brother.

**Additional examples:**

> Don't worry, everything will be fine.
> She will definitely pass the exam.
> I'll bet it will rain tomorrow.

**How to Recognize:** The speaker is expressing confidence or certainty about something.

**Contraction:** "Will" is often shortened to "'ll" in spoken English.

> I'll = I will
> He'll = He will
> They'll = They will
> That'll = That will

---

#### Use 3: Giving Orders or Making Requests

> **Will** you be quiet? (order/request)
> Come with me, **will** you? (request)

**Additional examples:**

> Will you close the door, please?
> Will you stop talking?
> Will you hand me that book?

**Note:** "Will you..." for requests can sound demanding. "Could you..." or "Would you..." are more polite.

---

#### Use 4: Asking About Wishes

Asking someone what they want.

> What **will** you have?
> What **will** you drink tonight?

**Additional examples:**

> What will you order?
> What will you wear to the party?
> What will you do this weekend?

**Note:** In casual speech, people often use "What do you want?" instead.

---

#### Use 5: Willingness and Decision

Expressing that you are willing to do something or have decided to do it.

> "Can you help me?" -> "I **will**." (I'm willing to help.)
> I **will** be there for you.
> She **will** help you with the project.

**Additional examples:**

> "Who wants to go first?" -> "I will!"
> I'll take care of it.
> He will do whatever it takes.

**Structure:**

> Subject + will + base verb

---

### 8.2 WOULD

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:** Use "would" for: polite requests, hypothetical situations, and past habits.

**Uses of "would" (from transcript):**

#### Use 1: Past of "Will" (Indirect Speech)

When converting direct speech with "will" to indirect speech.

**Direct:** She said, "I **will** come tomorrow."
**Indirect:** She said that she **would** come the next day.

**Additional examples:**

> Direct: "I will help you." -> Indirect: He said he would help me.
> Direct: "They will arrive soon." -> Indirect: They said they would arrive soon.

---

#### Use 2: Polite Requests

More polite than "will."

> **Would** you like some coffee?
> **Would** you mind closing the door?
> I **would** appreciate your help.

**Additional examples:**

> Would you pass me the salt?
> Would you mind if I sat here?
> Would you like to join us for dinner?

**Structure:**

> Would + you + base verb?

---

#### Use 3: Preferences and Wishes

Expressing what you would like or prefer.

> I **would** like a cup of tea.
> She **would** prefer to stay home.
> They **would** rather go to the beach.

**Additional examples:**

> I would like to book a table for two.
> Would you like to dance?
> I would prefer not to discuss this.

**Structure:**

> Subject + would like + to + base verb
> Subject + would rather + base verb

---

#### Use 4: Hypothetical or Unreal Situations

Used in conditional sentences (second and third conditionals) for imaginary or unlikely situations.

> If I had more time, I **would** travel the world.
> If I were rich, I **would** buy a house.
> If she had studied, she **would** have passed.

**Additional examples:**

> If I won the lottery, I would donate to charity.
> If I were you, I would accept the offer.
> If they had left earlier, they would have arrived on time.

**Structure:**

> If + past tense, would + base verb (second conditional)
> If + past perfect, would have + past participle (third conditional)

---

#### Use 5: Past Habits (Used to)

Expressing something that happened regularly in the past.

> When I was young, I **would** play cricket every day.
> She **would** always sit in the corner.
> We **would** go to the beach every summer.

**Additional examples:**

> My grandmother would tell us stories at bedtime.
> He would walk to school every morning.
> They would celebrate every festival together.

**Note:** "Would" for past habits implies a repeated action, not a single event.

---

#### Use 6: Willingness in the Past

> He **would** help anyone who asked.
> She **would** never say no to a challenge.

**Additional examples:**

> My father would always support us.
> They would come whenever we invited them.

---

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER — WILL vs WOULD:**

| Feature | WILL | WOULD |
|---------|------|-------|
| Time | Present/Future | Past/Hypothetical |
| Politeness | Normal | More polite |
| Requests | Will you...? | Would you...? |
| Hypothetical | No | Yes (if + past, would...) |
| Past habits | No | Yes (would + base verb) |
| Example | I will help. | I would help. |

**Memory Trick:**
- "Will" = real, present/future
- "Would" = imaginary, past, or more polite

---

### 8.4 Want vs Will — Important Distinction

The transcript makes an important distinction:

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:**

| WANT | WILL |
|------|------|
| Expresses a wish/thought | Expresses an action/decision |
| Stays in imagination | Implies doing something |
| Example: I want to be healthy. (wish only) | Example: I will be healthy. (I'll take action) |
| Example: Do you want me to open the door? (uncertain) | Example: Will you open the door? (action expected) |

**Key Difference:**

- **Want** = desire, no commitment to action
- **Will** = intention, commitment to action

> "I **want** to learn French." (I desire it, but may not do anything about it.)
> "I **will** learn French." (I've decided to do it.)

## 10. Confusing Concepts — Comparison Tables

### 9.1 Since vs For

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:**

| SINCE | FOR |
|-------|-----|
| Starting point (specific time/date) | Duration (period of time) |

⚡ **Practical Rule:**

> **Since = starting point**
> **For = duration**

Examples:

> I've lived here **since 2022**.
> I've lived here **for 4 years**.

**How to Recognize:**

- Is it a specific date, year, or moment? -> **since**
- Is it a number of hours, days, months, years? -> **for**

**Additional examples:**

| Since | For |
|-------|-----|
| since Monday | for three days |
| since 2020 | for two years |
| since morning | for six hours |
| since last week | for a long time |

**Common Mistake:**

❌ "I have been waiting since two hours."
✅ "I have been waiting **for** two hours."

❌ "I am working for Monday."
✅ "I am working **since** Monday."

---

### 9.2 Gone vs Went

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:**

| GONE | WENT |
|------|------|
| Needs auxiliary verb before it | No auxiliary needed |

⚡ **Practical Rule:**

> **"Gone" needs a helper (has/had/is/was). "Went" stands alone.**

**How to Recognize:**

- Is there a helping verb (had, has, have, is, was) before it? -> **gone**
- Is it the main verb with no helper? -> **went**

**Additional examples:**

| Gone (with auxiliary) | Went (no auxiliary) |
|-----------------------|---------------------|
| He has gone to the store. | He went to the store. |
| They had gone home. | They went home. |
| She has gone abroad. | She went abroad. |

**Common Mistake:**

❌ "She gone to school." (no auxiliary)
✅ "She **went** to school." OR "She **has gone** to school."

---

### 9.3 Its vs It's

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:**

| ITS | IT'S |
|-----|------|
| Possessive (shows ownership) | Contraction of "it is" |

⚡ **Practical Rule:**

> **Can you replace it with "it is"? Then use it's.**
> **Does it show ownership (like his/hers)? Then use its.**

**Additional examples:**

| Its (possessive) | It's (it is) |
|------------------|--------------|
| The dog wagged its tail. | It's raining outside. |
| The company lost its CEO. | It's time to go. |
| The tree lost its leaves. | It's a beautiful morning. |

**Common Mistake:**

❌ "Its a great day." (missing apostrophe)
✅ "**It's** a great day."

❌ "The cat licked it's paw." (unnecessary apostrophe)
✅ "The cat licked **its** paw."

**Memory Trick:**

- **It's** = **it is** (if you can expand it to "it is", use the apostrophe)
- **Its** = **his/hers/theirs** (possessive, no apostrophe)

---

### 9.4 Blunder vs Mistake

🟡 **UNDERSTAND:** Both mean error, but "blunder" implies carelessness, while "mistake" is more accidental.

| BLUNDER | MISTAKE |
|---------|---------|
| Due to **carelessness** | **Accidental** / unintentional |
| Example: A last-minute **blunder** cost them the match. | Example: I called him on his phone **by mistake**. |

**Additional examples:**

| Blunder | Mistake |
|---------|---------|
| The chef made a blunder by adding salt instead of sugar. | By mistake, I took his umbrella. |
| His blunder in the exam cost him his scholarship. | I made a mistake in the calculation. |
| The team's blunder led to their defeat. | Sorry, I sent the email by mistake. |

**How to Recognize:**

- Was it due to carelessness? -> **blunder**
- Was it accidental/unintentional? -> **mistake**

**Note:** Both are errors, but "blunder" implies more responsibility/carelessness.

---

### 9.5 Adjective vs Adverb

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:**

| ADJECTIVE | ADVERB |
|-----------|--------|
| Describes a **noun** | Describes a **verb** |

⚡ **Practical Rule:**

> **After a verb, use an -ly word (adverb), not a plain adjective.**
> "She sings **beautifully**" NOT "She sings beautiful."

**How to Recognize:**

- Is it describing a person/thing? -> **adjective**
- Is it describing an action? -> **adverb**

**Additional examples:**

| Adjective | Adverb |
|-----------|--------|
| quick | quickly |
| slow | slowly |
| happy | happily |
| angry | angrily |
| good | well |

**Common Mistake:**

❌ "She sings beautiful." (adjective where adverb is needed)
✅ "She sings **beautifully**."

❌ "He drives careful." (adjective where adverb is needed)
✅ "He drives **carefully**."

**Exception:** Some adverbs look like adjectives: fast, hard, early

> He runs **fast**. (fast is both adjective and adverb)
> She works **hard**. (hard is both adjective and adverb)

---

### 9.6 Who vs Whom

🟡 **UNDERSTAND:** In formal English, "who" = subject, "whom" = object. In everyday speech, most people use "who" for both. But "whom" is correct in formal writing.

| WHO | WHOM |
|-----|------|
| Subject of the sentence | Object of the sentence |
| Replaces he/she/they | Replaces him/her/them |
| Example: **Who** is coming? (He is coming.) | Example: **Whom** did you call? (You called him.) |

**Quick Test:** Replace with he/she (who) or him/her (whom).

> **Who** said that? -> **He** said that. (who is correct)
> **Whom** do you trust? -> You trust **him**. (whom is correct)

---

### 9.7 Which vs That

🟡 **UNDERSTAND:** In formal grammar, "which" = extra info (non-restrictive), "that" = essential info (restrictive). In everyday speech, people often use "that" for both.

| WHICH | THAT |
|-------|------|
| Used with **non-restrictive** clauses (extra info) | Used with **restrictive** clauses (essential info) |
| Needs commas | No commas |
| Example: The car, **which** was red, sped away. | Example: The car **that** I bought is fast. |

**Additional examples:**

| Which | That |
|-------|------|
| My phone, which I bought last week, is broken. | The phone that I bought is broken. |
| The book, which was on the table, is mine. | The book that is on the table is mine. |

**Quick Test:**

- Can you remove the clause and the sentence still makes sense? -> **which**
- Is the clause essential to identify the noun? -> **that**

## 11. Common Grammar Mistakes

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:** These are the most common mistakes to actively watch for.

### Mistake 1: Using "Myself" in Introductions

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| Myself Ram. | I am Ram. / I'm Ram. |
| Myself Ash, and I come from Bangalore. | I am Ash, and I come from Bangalore. |

**Why?** "Myself" is a reflexive pronoun. It should only be used when the subject and object are the same.

> I hurt **myself**. (correct - subject "I" and object "myself" are same)
> **Myself** Ram. (incorrect - no subject for "myself" to refer back to)

---

### Mistake 2: Using Abbreviations in Formal Writing

| Incorrect (formal) | Correct (formal) |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| gn (good night) | good night |
| h r u? (how are you?) | How are you? |
| thx (thanks) | thank you |
| plz (please) | please |
| msg (message) | message |

**Note:** Abbreviations are acceptable in informal texting/chatting, but NOT in formal communication, emails, or professional settings.

---

### Mistake 3: Confusing "Since" and "For"

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| I have been waiting since two hours. | I have been waiting **for** two hours. |
| I am working for Monday. | I am working **since** Monday. |

---

### Mistake 4: Using "Can" for Future Probability

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| There can be a seminar next week. | There **may/might** be a seminar next week. |
| We can go running tomorrow. | We **may/might** go running tomorrow. |

---

### Mistake 5: Using "Could" for One-Time Past Events

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| I could throw the stick 10 feet yesterday. | I **managed to** throw the stick 10 feet yesterday. |
| How many chocolates could you get? | How many chocolates **were you able to** get? |

---

### Mistake 6: Using "By" Instead of "To" or "With" in Passive Voice

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| He is known by her. | He is known **to** her. |
| The glass is filled by water. | The glass is filled **with** water. |

---

### Mistake 7: Using "Am Going To" Incorrectly in Indirect Speech

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| Ram said that he **was going** to cook. | Ram said that he **is going** to cook. OR Ram said that he **was going** to cook. |

**Note:** Both "is going to" and "was going to" are acceptable in indirect speech for "am going to."

---

### Mistake 8: Double Negatives

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| I don't have no money. | I don't have **any** money. |
| She can't hardly wait. | She can **hardly** wait. |

---

### Mistake 9: Subject-Verb Agreement

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| She don't know. | She **doesn't** know. |
| They doesn't care. | They **don't** care. |
| He go to school. | He **goes** to school. |

---

### Mistake 10: Wrong Preposition

| Incorrect | Correct |
|-----------|---------|
| I will meet to him. | I will meet **him**. (no preposition) |
| She is good in English. | She is good **at** English. |
| He depends of his parents. | He depends **on** his parents. |

---

### Mistake 11: Confusing "Say" and "Tell"

| SAY | TELL |
|-----|------|
| No object directly after | Takes an object directly |
| He said **that** he was tired. | He told **me** that he was tired. |
| She said **to** him... (informal) | She told **him**... |

⚡ **Practical Rule:**

> **say** = say + (to someone) + that clause
> **tell** = tell + someone + that clause

**Common Mistake:**

❌ "He said me that he was tired."
✅ "He **told** me that he was tired." OR "He said **to** me that he was tired."

---

### Mistake 12: Confusing "Speak" and "Talk"

| SPEAK | TALK |
|-------|------|
| More formal | More casual |
| Can be one-directional | Usually two-directional |
| "May I **speak** to the manager?" | "Let's **talk** about this." |

### Key Grammar Rules

#### Sentences & Clauses
- Clause = Subject + Verb
- Independent clause = complete sense
- Dependent clause = incomplete on its own

#### Parts of Speech
- Noun = person/place/thing/idea
- Pronoun = replaces noun (I, he, she, it, they)
- Verb = action or state of being
- Adjective = describes noun (beautiful, red, tall)
- Adverb = describes verb/adj/adv (quickly, very)
- Conjunction = connects (and, but, because)
- Preposition = position/time (in, on, at)
- Interjection = emotion (wow, alas)

#### Active vs Passive Voice
- Active: Subject + Verb + Object (She eats an apple)
- Passive: Object + is/was + V3 + by + Subject (An apple is eaten by her)
- Use "to" for: known, married, etc.
- Use "with" for: filled, covered, etc.

#### Direct vs Indirect Speech
- Add "that" connector
- Change pronoun (I -> he/she)
- Change tense (present -> past)
- Change time words (today -> that day)
- Exception: If reporting verb is present tense, no tense change

## What I Actually Need to Remember

---

### Tenses

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:**

**Simple Present:** Actions that happen regularly
> I **work** every day.
> She **doesn't** work on Sundays.

**Simple Past:** Actions that happened and finished
> I **worked** yesterday.
> She **didn't** come.

**Present Perfect:** Past action connected to now
> I **have worked** here for 3 years.
> She **has already left**.

**Present Perfect Continuous:** Ongoing action that started in the past
> I **have been working** since morning.
> She **has been waiting** for an hour.

**Future (will):** Predictions, promises, decisions
> I **will call** you tomorrow.
> She **will be** 25 next year.

⚡ **Practical Rules:**

> **Don't use "since/for" with simple past.**
> ❌ I live here since 2020.
> ✅ I **have lived** here since 2020.

> **Don't use specific finished time with present perfect.**
> ❌ I have finished yesterday.
> ✅ I finished yesterday. / I have finished already.

---

### Articles (a, an, the)

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:**

> **a** = one, non-specific (a book, a car)
> **an** = one, non-specific, before vowel sounds (an apple, an hour)
> **the** = specific (the book on the table)

⚡ **Practical Rules:**

> Use **a/an** for first mention: "I saw **a** dog."
> Use **the** for second mention: "**The** dog was brown."
> Use **the** when both people know which one: "Pass me **the** salt."

⚪ **DON'T MEMORIZE:** The detailed rules about "the" with geographical names, unique objects, etc. Just know the basics above.

---

### Prepositions

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:**

> **in** = inside a space / larger periods (in the room, in January)
> **on** = on a surface / days (on the table, on Monday)
> **at** = specific point (at the door, at 5 PM)

**Place:** in, on, at, under, between, behind, near, across, through, over, to, from

**Time:** at, on, in, since, for, until, during, before, after

⚡ **Practical Rules:**

> **Since** = starting point (since 2020)
> **For** = duration (for 3 years)
> **Depend on** (not "depend of")
> **Interested in** (not "interested of")
> **Good at** (not "good in" for skills)

---

### Modals

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:**

| Modal | Main Uses | Example |
|-------|-----------|---------|
| **can** | ability, permission | I **can** swim. / **Can** I go? |
| **could** | past ability, polite | I **could** swim at 5. / **Could** you help? |
| **may** | chance, permission (I/we) | It **may** rain. / **May** I sit? |
| **might** | smaller chance | It **might** rain. |
| **will** | future, certainty | I **will** help. |
| **would** | polite, hypothetical | **Would** you like coffee? |

**Key Differences:**

> **can vs could:** can = now, could = past or more polite
> **may vs might:** may = more likely (~50%), might = less likely (~30%)
> **will vs would:** will = real future, would = imaginary or polite

⚡ **Practical Rules:**

> **Don't use "can" for future chances.** Use "may" or "might."
> ❌ There can be a meeting.
> ✅ There **may/might** be a meeting.

> **Don't use "could" for one-time past achievements.** Use "managed to" or "were able to."
> ❌ I could solve the puzzle yesterday.
> ✅ I **managed to** solve the puzzle yesterday.

> **"Could have + V3" for missed opportunities/criticism.**
> You **could have helped** me. (But you didn't.)

### Subject-Verb Agreement

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:**

> **He/She/It** takes **does/doesn't/is/was/has**
> **I/You/We/They** take **do/don't/are/were/have**

⚡ **Practical Rules:**

> **She doesn't** (not "she don't")
> **They don't** (not "they doesn't")
> **He goes** (not "he go")
> **Everyone is** (not "everyone are")

**Common Mistakes:**

| Wrong | Right |
|-------|-------|
| She don't know | She **doesn't** know |
| They doesn't care | They **don't** care |
| He go to school | He **goes** to school |
| The team are winning | The team **is** winning |

### Sentence Structure

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:**

**Active Voice:**
> Subject + Verb + Object
> **She** eats **an apple**.

**Passive Voice:**
> Object + be + V3 + by + Subject
> **An apple** is eaten **by her**.

**Direct to Indirect Speech:**
> Add "that" + change pronoun + change tense + change time words

**Polite Requests:**
> **Could you** + base verb?
> **Would you** + base verb?
> **I would like** + to + base verb.

🔴 **MUST REMEMBER:**

| Word A | Word B | Difference |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| **say** | **tell** | say + that / tell + someone + that |
| **speak** | **talk** | speak = formal / talk = casual |
